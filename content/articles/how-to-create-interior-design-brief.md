A Pinterest board can reveal taste quickly. It can show that you return to warm oak, quiet stone, low furniture, generous curtains and rooms that feel calm rather than styled. What it cannot show is whether the sofa fits through the door, which wall must keep a radiator, how the room is used at 7 a.m., or what “warm” means when two people imagine it differently.

That gap is where expensive misunderstandings begin.

An interior design brief turns visual attraction into a set of usable decisions. It gives a designer, contractor or design tool the context needed to solve the right problem. It also gives you a standard against which to assess suggestions: not simply “Do I like this image?” but “Does this proposal answer the room, routines, constraints and priorities we agreed?”

This guide shows how to build that brief without pretending to be your own architect. The aim is not a construction document. It is a clear homeowner record of what is true, what matters, what is still unknown and what a successful design must achieve.

## Start with the Decision, Not the Style

Before collecting more images, finish this sentence:

> We are changing this room because…

A useful answer describes a lived problem. “The living room looks dated” is broad. “Four people cannot sit comfortably without blocking the route to the patio, and evening light is either harsh or too dim for reading” gives the project something to solve.

Write one primary outcome and no more than three supporting outcomes. If every wish is treated as equally urgent, the brief cannot guide a trade-off.

| Weak starting point | More useful brief statement |
|---|---|
| Make it luxurious | Create a calm evening room with comfortable seating for four and one clear garden route |
| Add storage | Give everyday bags, chargers and paperwork a defined home near where they are used |
| Improve the lighting | Support reading, conversation and low-level evening movement without relying on one bright ceiling source |
| Modernise the kitchen | Improve preparation flow and storage while retaining the sound cabinetry and existing service positions where practical |

The statement does not dictate a solution. It identifies what the solution must do.

## Record How the Room Really Works

Design photographs show a frozen moment. Your brief should show the day.

Walk through the room at ordinary times and record activities, people, objects and friction. Include the untidy evidence. A pile of shoes beside the door is not a failure to hide from the designer; it is evidence that the room needs an arrival solution.

For each recurring activity, note:

- who does it and when;
- what furniture, power, light, privacy or storage it needs;
- what interrupts it now;
- what must remain accessible;
- what a comfortable result would feel like.

A living room brief may include early-morning coffee, children's play, weekend visitors, television viewing and evening reading. A kitchen brief may include school-lunch preparation, two people cooking together, grocery unloading and where small appliances are actually used. The [kitchen layout guide](/article/kitchen-design-layout/) can help turn those routines into zones without prescribing cabinetry too early.

Do not write for an imaginary, permanently tidy household. The strongest brief describes the home you live in and the amount of maintenance you will realistically accept.

## Make a Reliable Existing-Conditions Pack

The room must be documented before it is interpreted. Create one simple pack containing photographs, a rough plan and a measured schedule.

### Photograph the whole room systematically

Stand in every corner and take overlapping photographs at ordinary eye level. Then photograph each wall straight on. Add closer views of doors, windows, outlets, switches, vents, radiators, plumbing points, built-ins, thresholds and visible damage.

Give files plain names such as `living-room-north-wall` and `patio-door-threshold`. A glamorous image is less useful than a complete sequence that lets someone understand what is outside the frame.

### Measure without implying false precision

Record overall wall lengths, ceiling height, openings, sill heights, door swings, fixed furniture and obvious service positions. Note floor-level changes and the narrowest route through which large items must pass.

Use a consistent unit. Mark every homeowner dimension as preliminary. Critical dimensions for joinery, structural work, services or orders should be verified by the responsible professional or supplier.

Measure twice from different reference points. If two readings disagree, record the uncertainty rather than choosing the more convenient number. A hand sketch with honest question marks is safer than a polished plan built on one incorrect dimension.

### Add the conditions a photograph cannot prove

Note morning and afternoon sun, glare, drafts, noise, damp concerns, hot or cold zones, Wi-Fi weakness and how privacy changes after dark. Record observations, not diagnoses. “Paint bubbles beneath the west window after heavy rain” is useful; “the wall has rising damp” is a technical conclusion that requires investigation.

## Decide What Stays, What Changes and What Is Uncertain

Create three lists.

**Must stay** may include sound flooring, a valued table, original joinery, a working fireplace or a view that should remain open.

**May change** covers items that can move or be replaced if doing so clearly improves the outcome.

**Unknown** is equally important. It may include whether a wall is structural, whether wiring can support a proposed control, what lies beneath the floor or whether a fabric is still available.

This prevents two common mistakes: designing around something nobody values, and quietly assuming that an important object can disappear.

For retained pieces, record actual dimensions, photographs, condition and the reason for keeping them. “Keep the oak cabinet” is weaker than “Keep the 1,480 mm oak cabinet because it stores family records and establishes the warm timber direction; moving it is acceptable, repainting it is not.”

## Edit Pinterest Inspiration into Evidence

Keep the board, but reduce its job. It should communicate recurring design signals, not act as a shopping list.

Start by grouping images into themes: layout, proportion, materials, light, colour, storage, architectural detail and atmosphere. Then look for repetition. If twelve saved rooms contain low, pale sofas and only one contains a tall velvet sectional, the repeated pattern is stronger evidence than the dramatic outlier.

Choose a small working set and annotate each image with two sentences:

1. **What specifically works?** Name the element: low horizontal furniture, concealed curtain track, warm wall wash, deep window reveal or quiet material palette.
2. **How might it translate here?** Connect the idea to the real room, or state why it may not.

Also include a few “not this” examples. Explain the boundary: too glossy, visually crowded, cold at night, difficult to maintain, or dependent on ceiling height the room does not have.

Avoid statements such as “I love everything about this.” No room is transferable in its entirety. The architecture, camera lens, daylight, styling budget and editing all affect what you see.

### Convert adjectives into observable choices

Words such as *warm*, *minimal*, *timeless* and *luxurious* are useful only after they are translated.

| Abstract word | Observable direction |
|---|---|
| Warm | Cream rather than blue-white walls; oak or warm timber; 2700K–3000K samples assessed in the actual room where appropriate |
| Calm | One clear focal point; restrained contrast; closed storage for daily clutter; visible surfaces left partly empty |
| Natural | Honest texture and variation; material samples reviewed in daylight and evening light |
| Minimal | Fewer visible objects, but sufficient concealed storage and practical charging locations |
| Timeless | Permanent finishes chosen for the house and maintenance tolerance, with trend expression kept to easier-to-change layers |

The [luxury interior design principles guide](/article/luxury-interior-design-principles/) can help identify proportion, restraint, light and material qualities. Your brief should then state which of those principles matter in this project.

## Define Functional and Aesthetic Requirements Separately

Separating the two stops visual decisions from hiding practical failures.

Functional requirements might include seating count, accessible storage, reading light, blackout, cable management, acoustic comfort, ventilation access and a clear circulation route. Aesthetic direction may cover visual weight, palette, material character, contrast and the desired atmosphere at different times.

They meet later. A curtain is both functional and visual; a lamp affects both task performance and atmosphere. But the brief should first make each requirement visible so that a beautiful proposal cannot quietly omit it.

### Use products to test the brief, not to replace it

A real product can expose missing requirements. If the brief proposes motorised shading, the existing [roller-blind motor candidate](https://steynenslin.s.gy/roller-blind-motor) is useful as a specification exercise: its current mapping is for a battery-powered motor for a 38 mm tube. That prompts the right questions about tube size, blind weight, charging access, controls, manual override and compatibility. It is not proof that the product suits your window; confirm the current listing and installation requirements before considering it.

For lighting, a [rechargeable picture light](https://steynenslin.s.gy/picture-light) can illustrate a low-commitment accent-light test, while a [smart dimmer](https://steynenslin.s.gy/smart-dimmer) illustrates why circuit, neutral-wire, minimum-load, lamp and driver compatibility belong in the brief. These physical items help test individual execution decisions. They do not replace the complete project brief.

## Set Priorities, Budget Boundaries and Decision Rules

A brief without priorities is a wish list. Label requirements:

- **essential** — the project fails without it;
- **important** — strong preference, negotiable for a clear reason;
- **optional** — valuable only if budget, space and technical conditions allow;
- **excluded** — deliberately outside this phase.

Record the available project budget as a boundary or range, along with what it must include: professional fees, building work, furniture, window treatments, lighting, delivery, installation, tax and contingency. If the figure is not settled, say so and ask for options at defined levels. Hiding the budget does not create better value; it encourages proposals that cannot be compared realistically.

Add decision rules for predictable trade-offs. For example:

- preserve circulation before adding occasional seating;
- prefer serviceable fittings over sealed products where the appearance is comparable;
- retain sound materials rather than replacing them for a minor colour difference;
- spend first on the daily-use problem, then on styling layers.

These rules help when two options both look good.

## Write Constraints Without Designing the Solution

Constraints sharpen a design when they are factual. They become harmful when a homeowner prescribes an unverified technical answer.

Useful constraints include the tenancy or body-corporate rules, fixed project dates, access limits, household allergies, a no-drilling requirement, items that must remain operational and areas children or pets can reach.

Instead of writing “remove this wall,” write “create a stronger visual and conversational connection between the kitchen and living area; any structural change requires professional assessment and approval.” Instead of “install six downlights,” write “provide comfortable preparation light without exposed glare; propose and verify the lighting method.”

The brief owns the outcome and context. Qualified professionals own the technical design, compliance and verification appropriate to their role.

## Specify the Outputs You Need

Misunderstandings often concern deliverables rather than design taste. State what you expect at each stage.

A small room project might require:

1. an existing-conditions review and list of missing information;
2. one or two concept directions with the reasoning behind them;
3. a furniture and circulation plan;
4. a material and colour direction using physical samples where relevant;
5. a lighting intent and switching or scene concept;
6. a schedule of proposed items with dimensions and evidence still to verify;
7. drawings, specifications and approvals appropriate to the agreed professional scope.

Clarify the format, number of revisions, who supplies measurements, who coordinates contractors, what is excluded and which decisions require written approval. Do not assume a moodboard includes a measured layout or that a concept package includes construction documentation.

## Create Approval Criteria Before Reviewing Concepts

Approval criteria make feedback more useful than “It does not feel right.” Build a short scorecard from the brief.

| Question | Evidence to review |
|---|---|
| Does the room support the agreed activities? | Layout with furniture in occupied positions and circulation shown |
| Are retained items integrated? | Dimensions and locations checked against the proposal |
| Does the atmosphere match the brief? | Annotated views, samples and lighting intent in daytime and evening terms |
| Are unknowns still visible? | Assumptions and verification actions listed, not silently resolved |
| Is the proposal affordable at the agreed scope? | Like-for-like cost summary with exclusions and allowances |
| Can it be maintained? | Care, access, replacement and service implications discussed |

When giving feedback, tie it to an agreed criterion: “The second chair narrows the only garden route” is actionable. “I do not like it” is not yet a diagnosis.

## Common Briefing Mistakes

### Sending hundreds of unedited images

Volume hides the pattern. Curate and annotate the evidence that changes a decision.

### Describing only the desired look

The room still needs to support real people, objects, maintenance and movement. Include the day, not only the photograph.

### Treating preliminary dimensions as construction information

Homeowner measurements are valuable context. They should not be used as unverified fabrication dimensions.

### Concealing disagreement in the household

Record different priorities early. A designer can work with an explicit tension between display and concealed storage; discovering it after approval is expensive.

### Naming products without recording why

A product link can disappear or change. Capture the relevant type, dimensions, finish, performance requirement and evidence needed, not only the marketplace URL.

### Asking for concepts before defining success

Without a brief, every concept is judged against criteria that appear after the work is done.

## A One-Page Interior Design Brief Structure

Use this as the opening page, with evidence attached behind it:

1. **Project and room:** name, address or project identifier, current phase.
2. **Reason for change:** one clear problem statement.
3. **Primary outcome:** the most important result.
4. **Users and routines:** who, when and what the room must support.
5. **Existing conditions:** measurements, photographs, services and known issues.
6. **Must stay / may change / unknown:** explicit boundaries.
7. **Functional requirements:** storage, light, privacy, circulation and access.
8. **Aesthetic direction:** annotated recurring signals and dislikes.
9. **Priorities and exclusions:** essential, important, optional and out of scope.
10. **Budget and timing:** what is included, dependencies and decision dates.
11. **Required outputs:** stages, formats, revisions and responsibilities.
12. **Approval criteria:** how the proposal will be assessed.

The supporting pack may be long. The opening page should remain clear enough that everyone can return to it when a decision becomes difficult.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What should an interior design brief include?

Include the room's purpose, users and routines; measured existing conditions; what stays and changes; priorities, budget and timing; functional and aesthetic direction; constraints; required outputs; and clear approval criteria.

### Can I use a Pinterest board as an interior design brief?

A Pinterest board is useful evidence of taste, but it is not a complete brief. Annotate each important image to explain the specific proportion, material, light, layout or feeling you want, then connect it to the realities of your room.

### How many inspiration images should I give a designer?

A small edited set is usually more useful than hundreds of saves. Choose enough images to show recurring patterns and a few dislikes, then explain what each image proves. Remove images that add no new information.

### Do I need exact measurements before contacting a designer?

You need reliable baseline dimensions and photographs, but the responsible professional should verify critical measurements before drawings, fabrication or ordering. Label homeowner measurements as preliminary rather than presenting them as construction dimensions.

### How do I brief a contractor without prescribing the wrong solution?

Separate the outcome from the method. Describe what must work, what must be retained, known site conditions and the evidence required for approval, while leaving technical design and compliance decisions to the appropriately qualified professional.
