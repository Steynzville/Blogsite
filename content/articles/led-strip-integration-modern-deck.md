## Quick Takeaways

- Choose the **location and lighting effect first**, then the strip. A softly illuminated deck edge needs a different solution from a step, path or wet-area fixture.
- For a permanent deck or pergola, a **12V/24V system with a correctly specified outdoor driver** is often easier to integrate than a mains-voltage strip; a qualified installer may still be needed.
- **IP ratings apply to tested enclosures**, not automatically to every cut end, connector, driver or joint in the finished installation.
- **Run length is not a universal 10 m or 50 m rule**. Check watts per metre, driver capacity, feed-cable length and the manufacturer's maximum feed length.
- **COB is not a substitute for a diffuser**. Hide the strip from seated sightlines, manage glare and keep the light on the surface you want to see.
- Solar can work for modest decorative accents, but its winter runtime and charging conditions need realistic expectations.

## Start With the Surface, Not the Product

Before comparing prices, stand where you will actually sit at night. Can you see the strip itself, or only the deck edge it illuminates? Is there a step where feet need to land safely? Does water collect in the proposed channel? Is there a protected location for the power supply?

Use this decision table as a starting point, **not an electrical specification**:

| Your situation | First option to investigate | What must be checked |
|---|---|---|
| Deck rail or pergola with an accessible outdoor power point | Outdoor-rated 12V/24V LED strip with a compatible driver | Actual power draw, cable route, driver location, weather-rated connectors and glare |
| Long permanent architectural run as part of building work | Professionally designed mains-rated or distributed low-voltage system | Local electrical code, qualified installation, product-specific maximum run and maintenance access |
| Remote, low-output accent with good solar exposure | Complete solar lighting kit | Realistic battery runtime, winter sun, panel location and replaceable parts |
| Exposed walking surface or steps | Dedicated rated step/path fixture | Slip/trip risks, mounting and drainage rather than a bare strip |

**Three measurements to take:** total illuminated length; distance from power supply to the first strip; and the exact location of every connection. Photograph the installation from seated eye level before choosing a diffuser profile. These details influence the result more than a headline LED count.

## Compare Three Ways to Power Outdoor LED Strips

### 1. Mains-Voltage Strip: A Specialist Option

Some purpose-designed 220–240V strip systems support long runs, but **longer does not mean automatically safer or suitable for every deck**. They have manufacturer-specific cut points, connectors, permitted mounting methods and protection requirements. Do not assume any generic marketplace strip can be cut, buried, recessed or connected directly to a socket.

**Consider when:** a qualified electrician is already planning permanent architectural lighting and can verify the exact system's approvals, outdoor exposure, run limits and local code requirements.

**Trade-off:** professional installation, limited flexibility in future alterations and more demanding protection against damage and water. A mains strip is not a default upgrade for a run longer than 10 metres.

### 2. 12V/24V DC Strip: A Flexible Planning Starting Point

A separate compatible power supply converts mains electricity to low-voltage DC. This makes it practical to divide a deck into shorter, controlled sections, but the power supply and its mains connection still need correct outdoor protection. A plug-in kit is not the same thing as permission to install fixed electrical wiring yourself.

**Consider when:** you want subtle lighting under a rail, beneath a bench or in a pergola channel, with a weather-appropriate driver location and a clear maintenance route.

**Trade-off:** voltage drop and heat become important as wattage, cable length and strip length increase. The manufacturer's feed-length table matters more than a universal rule. Where appropriate, a designer may specify separate feeds or additional power injection rather than one continuous run.

### 3. Solar Strip: An Accent, Not a Guaranteed Substitute

A complete solar kit can be useful where power is genuinely inaccessible, especially for a small decorative feature. The panel needs a location that receives adequate direct sun; the battery must supply the strip through the desired evening hours. Shade, short winter days and ageing batteries can sharply reduce runtime.

**Consider when:** a remote accent can tolerate variable brightness and you can position the panel without an awkward visible cable.

**Trade-off:** battery capacity, solar panel output and real-world runtime vary widely. Avoid generic claims such as '20–30 hours from a short charge' unless the exact product's tested conditions substantiate them. Do not assume a sealed solar strip can be cut or daisy-chained.

## COB, SMD and Diffusers: Design the Visible Result

COB (chip-on-board) strips often create a smoother apparent line of light than lower-density SMD (surface-mount device) strips. That is useful when the strip is near the edge of a visible channel, but the distinction is not absolute: SMD density, viewing distance, diffuser depth and surface reflectance all change whether individual points are visible.

| Question | More useful than a label |
|---|---|
| Will people see the strip directly? | Recess it or shield the viewing angle |
| Will the surface show bright dots? | Test the actual strip behind the actual diffuser |
| Will the profile trap heat or water? | Follow the strip and channel manufacturer's mounting guidance |
| Is the light comfortable from a seat? | Test at night with the intended dimmer setting |

Choose a **softly illuminated surface**, not simply the strip with the highest LED count. A low-glare warm-white design often looks more intentional than a brighter line that competes with the garden.

## IP65, IP67 and the Weakest Point of the Installation

Ingress-protection (IP) codes are defined by **IEC 60529**. The second digit describes a particular water-exposure test; it does not guarantee a complete outdoor installation will stay dry indefinitely.

| Marking | Water test it describes | What it does **not** establish |
|---|---|---|
| IP65 | Protection against water jets under specified test conditions | Safe immersion, or a sealed cut end/driver |
| IP67 | Protection against temporary immersion under specified test conditions | Suitability for every hose jet, chemical, UV or permanent wet exposure |
| IP68 | Immersion performance under manufacturer-stated conditions | Automatic approval for a pond, pool or any installation method |

**IP67 is not simply 'better IP65' for every exposure.** If a location sees both jets and immersion, verify the manufacturer specifies protection against both. A rating on the silicone strip does not cover a field-cut end, connector, power supply or junction box unless those components and their assembly are rated accordingly.

For a deck, look at *where water will sit*, not only whether rain falls. Avoid channels that become gutters. Provide suitable cable routing, strain relief and access to inspect joints. Near pools and spas, use a qualified electrician and the applicable special-location rules.

Reference: [IEC 60529, Degrees of protection provided by enclosures](https://webstore.iec.ch/en/publication/2452).

## Plan the Run Length and Driver Before You Buy

A universal '24V works for 10 metres' rule is unreliable. The same length behaves differently with a 5 W/m strip and a 15 W/m strip, or with a short heavy cable versus a long thin one. The manufacturer's maximum feed length, voltage-drop guidance and thermal limits govern.

### A Worked Planning Example (Not an Installation Specification)

Suppose a pergola needs **6 metres** of a hypothetical **24V strip rated 8 W/m**:

1. Strip load = **6 m × 8 W/m = 48 W**.
2. Nominal strip current = **48 W ÷ 24 V = 2 A**.
3. A **60 W or larger compatible 24V constant-voltage driver** may offer planning headroom, *if* the manufacturer permits the continuous load, ambient temperature and mounting location. Do not select a driver on wattage alone.
4. Check the driver's outdoor rating, mains connection, permitted cable length and the strip maker's maximum single-feed run. Split into shorter independently fed sections if required.
5. Mark every cut, connector and cable entry on a sketch; verify how each will remain sealed and serviceable.

This calculation helps you ask the right questions. It is **not** permission to wire a mains supply or assume a particular product can run six metres from one feed.

Professional LED-strip documentation shows that feed-cable length and conductor cross-section can materially change allowable strip length: see [LEDVANCE's feed-length guidance](https://www.ledvance.com/00_Free_To_Use/asset-10795714_LEDVANCE_LED_Strip_System_-_Wave_2_-_Fall_2020_EN.pdf).

### Before You Order, Confirm These Six Details

- Exact voltage and **watts per metre** of the selected variant.
- Approved driver type, capacity, environmental rating and local mains compatibility.
- Manufacturer's maximum run per feed and permitted cable size/length.
- Whether cutting is allowed, at which marks, and how cut ends are resealed.
- Compatibility with the chosen channel, diffuser, dimmer and mounting surface.
- Access for maintenance, water drainage and protection from accidental damage.

## Installation: What You Can Plan and What Needs a Professional

For a **complete, manufacturer-approved plug-in low-voltage kit**, homeowners may be able to mount the supplied channel and fixtures where local rules permit. Read the exact instructions, use a suitably protected outdoor outlet, keep the power supply in its approved environment and never alter a sealed connector casually.

A qualified electrician should handle **new outlets, fixed wiring, mains-voltage strips, driver mains connections, uncertain wet-location work and installations near pools or spas**. A low-voltage LED strip still depends on equipment connected to the mains.

Before any permanent work, inspect the route in daylight and at night. Keep cables away from sharp edges, pinch points and standing water; leave access to replace the driver; and do not bury connectors inside inaccessible finishes. Test the assembled system according to the manufacturer's instructions before concealing it.

In the United States, the [Electrical Safety Foundation International](https://www.esfi.org/ground-fault-circuit-interrupters-gfci/) recommends ground-fault protection wherever water and electricity may meet, including outdoors. Elsewhere, follow the applicable local protection and installation requirements rather than assuming US rules apply universally.

## Seven Common Mistakes That Make Outdoor Strips Disappointing

1. **Buying by voltage alone.** Run length also depends on power per metre, feed cables and driver design.
2. **Trusting the strip's IP label for the whole system.** Connectors, cut ends and drivers need their own protection.
3. **Treating COB as automatically glare-free.** Shield the light source and test from normal viewing positions.
4. **Placing a channel where water pools.** Drainage and maintenance access are design decisions.
5. **Assuming all low-voltage work is DIY.** Fixed wiring and mains-side work require appropriate professional expertise.
6. **Cutting a sealed or mains strip without instructions.** Follow the exact product's approved cut and sealing method.
7. **Treating solar runtime as fixed.** Plan for seasonal sunlight, battery ageing and the hours you actually use the space.

## Maintenance: Inspect the Whole System

Check outdoor channels, joints and drivers after the first heavy rain and periodically thereafter. Look for water accumulation, discoloured connectors, loose fixings, damaged insulation or unexpectedly dim sections. Disconnect power and have damaged electrical parts assessed or replaced appropriately rather than applying sealant over a fault.

Clean exposed diffusers and solar panels according to the manufacturer; keep drainage paths open. LED lifetime ratings are measured under specified thermal and operating conditions and are **not** a promise of 10–20 maintenance-free years. Drivers, batteries, seals and connectors can fail before the LEDs do.

Keep a simple installation record: strip model, voltage, wattage, driver model, circuit, cut locations and purchase documents. It makes future repair far easier than guessing which anonymous strip was installed.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can I use an indoor LED strip outdoors?**  
Only if the complete product is expressly approved for the actual exposure. Check the driver, joints and cut ends as carefully as the strip itself.

**Is 24V always better than 12V?**  
No. It can simplify some longer runs, but the actual power draw, cable size, voltage drop and manufacturer's limits determine the layout.

**Do low-voltage LED strips need an electrician?**  
A complete outdoor-rated plug-in kit may be homeowner-installable where local rules allow. New mains connections, fixed wiring and uncertain wet-location installations need a qualified electrician.

**Is IP67 better than IP65?**  
They describe different water tests. Check the exact exposure and whether the complete assembly—not just the strip—meets it.

**Can I cut outdoor LED strips?**  
Only where and how the specific manufacturer permits, with the required sealing method. Do not assume a mains or solar strip is cuttable.

**How do I prevent LED dots and glare?**  
Use the right strip and diffuser, recess the source and test from normal seating positions at night.

**Will solar strips work in winter?**  
Sometimes, with reduced runtime. Compare the exact panel and battery specification with winter sun and the evening hours you need.

## The Bottom Line

For most deck and pergola projects, **start by planning a shielded, serviceable low-voltage lighting detail**, then check whether a compatible product and driver actually meet the measured run. Consider a professionally designed mains system only where its advantages justify the added installation requirements. Use solar where a variable-output accent is acceptable.

The decisive questions are not simply '12V, 24V or 220V?' They are: **Where will the light land? What will people see? How will the entire assembly stay safe and serviceable outdoors?**
